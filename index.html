Advisor: Gil Goldshlager

JAX · NumPy

Randomized Preconditioning for Neural Network Optimization

Undergraduate Researcher

Natural‑gradient methods for neural‑network wavefunctions require solving a damped Gram‑matrix system at every step. In float32 those systems are badly conditioned enough that the obvious approach, forming and solving the normal equations, diverges once the condition number passes roughly 107. That is not a hypothetical. It happens inside the training loop, and when it fails it fails silently, producing updates that look plausible and are not.

I implemented a randomized sketch‑and‑precondition solver using Cholesky‑QR, benchmarked against a float64 QR reference so that “more accurate” means something measurable. At moderate conditioning it is about 8× more accurate than the float32 baseline, and it stays finite out to condition numbers around 1015, where the baseline has long since blown up. The current work extends this from synthetic operators to the ones actually produced by neural networks, which is where the guarantees have to hold if the method is going to be trusted in the VMC training loop.

The experiment below sweeps the damping λ on a fixed operator, which is what controls the conditioning of the Gram matrix, and measures each solver against a float64 QR reference. Drag the slider from left to right and the float32 baseline simply stops: its Cholesky factorization fails and there is no answer to plot.

Two honest notes. The baseline does not return a wrong number past κ ≈ 107, it raises: the float32 Cholesky factorization fails because the matrix is no longer numerically positive definite. And the randomized solver staying finite is not the same as staying accurate. At κ ≈ 1015 its relative error is about 1.9, so the answer is unusable, it just does not blow up. The useful claim is the middle of the range, where the baseline has already failed and the randomized solve is still correct to four or five digits.

Smart Circle International

Newport Beach, CA

LangGraph · Snowflake · Python

AI Engineer Intern

The reporting workflow was the kind of problem that looks like a data‑entry chore and is actually a correctness problem. Order information arrived as GroupMe messages, got transcribed by hand, and fed weekly reports covering about a million dollars in sales. Errors were invisible until someone downstream noticed a number that felt wrong.

I rebuilt it as a LangGraph pipeline with a deliberate split. Deterministic field mapping handles anything with structure, and an LLM only handles the residue that doesn’t parse. That ordering matters, because the failure mode of an all‑LLM pipeline is confident nonsense on exactly the fields you can least afford to get wrong. Validation ran field by field against historical data, 5,418 fields, before it was allowed near a live report: 99.9% overall and zero errors on business‑critical fields.

The second project was inventory rebalancing across independent sales offices. Each office ordered in isolation, so shortages in one place coexisted with surplus in another. The pipeline pulls campaign and stock data from Snowflake three times a week, computes what each office actually needs, and matches shortfalls against same‑campaign surplus within a 50‑mile haversine radius, publishing the resulting transfers and reorders to ClickUp. It removed 8 to 10 hours a week of manual coordination.

Alongside both, I built and evaluated internal LLM tooling using Claude Code, retrieval, and structured prompting, working directly with the CIO.

Systematic QQQ Strategy & Signal Library

A daily leverage‑targeting strategy on QQQ. Thirty‑plus candidate signals across momentum, mean reversion, volatility regime, volume, and calendar effects, each written as a pure function from price data to a leverage series so any of them can be dropped into the backtester without touching it. The survivors combine into a composite scaled by a Garman‑Klass realized‑volatility anchor and squashed through tanh, so leverage is bounded smoothly rather than clipped.

The part I care about is the evaluation discipline. Leverage at close t applies to the t to t+1 return, which makes the backtest causal by construction, with no post‑hoc shift to forget. Costs are charged on every change in leverage. The benchmark is charged nothing, which handicaps the comparison against my own strategy on purpose.

The honest reading is that the edge is risk control rather than return generation. The volatility anchor de‑levers into turbulence, which costs participation in sharp rallies and buys shallower drawdowns. Validation Sharpe was 1.57, so the drop to 0.95 is the visible cost of having selected among thirty signals on that window, which is exactly why the holdout number is the one to believe.

Cross-Sectional Equity Factor Framework

Lookahead bias in factor research is rarely one dramatic mistake. It is three quiet ones: normalizing a signal using statistics that include the future, ranking a stock against peers that were not investable at the time, and applying the evaluation lag inconsistently across factors. Every one of them inflates backtest performance and none of them announce themselves.

This framework’s premise is that catching those by discipline does not scale. Two of the three are handled inside the engine where a factor author cannot reach them. Universe masking happens before cross‑sectional ranking, and there is exactly one shift(1) in the entire codebase, applied unconditionally. The third, normalization, is centralized behind a single module that exposes the only approved primitives, with a documented taxonomy of the unsafe pandas calls and why each one leaks.

The engine is fully vectorized with no Python‑level loops in the backtest path, built on runtime‑checkable Protocols and a frozen config so a new factor or data source is a drop‑in.

It documents its own limitations rather than hiding them. The static universe carries survivorship bias, normalization safety is currently enforced by convention rather than by the architecture, and there are no tests or CI yet. The README says so.

Kelly Criterion & Position-Sizing Simulator

A result that is easy to state and hard to feel: in a game where every individual bet has positive expected value, betting twice the Kelly fraction makes you lose money. Drag the sliders below and watch the two curves come apart.

The median path is the point. Above the Kelly fraction the average keeps climbing, carried by a handful of runs that ran away upward, while the typical run finishes below where it started. Averages are the wrong statistic for multiplicative processes, which is the whole lesson of Kelly sizing.

The curves are closed form: the average grows as (1 + f(2p‑1))t and the typical path as exp(t·g), where g is the expected log growth per round. The faint runs behind them are simulated on a fixed seed, so moving a slider changes the strategy and not the luck. In the repository, “probability of ruin” means P(final wealth ≤ $1 from a start of $100), since fixed‑fraction betting is multiplicative and capital never reaches exactly zero.

hyprland: Multi-Theme Wayland Desktop Engine

One config, swappable skins. hyprland.lua holds layout, keybinds, and behavior, and everything visual lives in themes/<name>/. Switching themes repoints a single relative symlink and seventeen surfaces follow: status bar, launcher, notification center, volume OSD, terminal, editor colorscheme, cursor set, lock screen, QML login greeter, and the rest. Themes are self‑describing directories. A theme.lua declares its own identity through keys read both by Lua at load and by shell scripts with sed, so the apply path has zero dependencies.

Two things in here are the actual engineering. The first is a compositor bug. Hyprland’s animated border gradient rotates once and freezes, a known upstream regression lineage still broken on 0.56.2 even though the animation registers. I worked around it with a userspace daemon that drives the rotation through live IPC evaluation, lifecycle‑managed so it starts only when the active theme asks for it and dies with the compositor. The first version ticked ten times a second and made things worse: every config write cancels in‑flight animations, so it was clipping every workspace slide. Correlating the failures against the daemon’s schedule identified the contention, and the fix was to slow the tick to once every two seconds and let the compositor’s own easing carry the motion.

The second is CI that encodes what debugging taught me. Beyond shellcheck and config validation, the pipeline fails the build on any non‑inset box-shadow or use of the transparent keyword in the stylesheets, because GTK3 composites an outer box‑shadow past the rounded edge of a layer‑shell surface, and that is how black halos got into the bar twice. The rule exists so it cannot happen a third time.

linux-setup: Idempotent Arch Provisioner

Setting up a machine the way you like it is a few hours of work you then repeat, badly, from memory, the next time. This repo makes it one command and makes it repeatable.

Package manifests are declarative, and installation retries per package so one bad AUR build doesn’t abort the run. Dotfiles are symlinked with timestamped backups of anything already there, and the script refuses to clobber symlinks another repo owns. Services and the firewall come up configured. There is an audit mode that diffs the live machine against the repo in both directions and reports drift, including orphaned packages, because the failure mode of a setup script is not the first run. It is the machine slowly diverging from it over six months.

Also

A coinflip risk simulator, the companion to the Kelly widget above. That one asks how much to bet when you have an edge; this one shows what happens with none. Fixed‑fraction betting on a fair coin still loses money at the median, $100 falling to $36.60 over 200 rounds at f = 0.10, which matches the closed‑form log‑growth rate of −0.005025 per round to the cent. A win and a loss of the same size leave you at (1+f)(1−f) = 1−f2, below where you started.
